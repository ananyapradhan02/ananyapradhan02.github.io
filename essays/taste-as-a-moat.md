Last Tuesday, I sat in a product strategy meeting to debate AI-generated campaign concepts. All three concepts were technically sound, all on-brand, all optimized for the distribution metrics we'd agreed to care about this quarter. Our design lead from the agency asked "Which one feels right?" And the room went quiet in a way that felt like an answer.

I've been thinking about that silence ever since.

The conversation around AI literacy tends to focus on the technical — understanding how models work, learning to write better prompts, staying current with whatever tool launched last week. But after months of watching teams (my own included) try to make sense of what these tools actually mean for our work, I've started to suspect we're solving the wrong problem. The question isn't how to use AI. It's how to know when what it gives you is worth keeping.

And that's not a technical problem. That's a taste problem.

I don't mean taste in the superficial sense — whether you prefer serif or sans-serif, minimalism or maximalism. I mean something closer to discernment. The ability to recognize when a machine has produced something that actually serves a human purpose versus something that merely resembles something that might.

Rick Rubin once said his job as a music producer isn't to understand music theory but to feel whether something is alive. I read that interview years ago, and I think about it constantly now. When AI can generate fifty variations of anything in the time it takes to refill your coffee, the question of aliveness becomes the only question that matters. Not: can this tool write my marketing copy? Obviously it can. But: can I tell when what it's written has any pulse at all?

Consider two campaigns, both using AI, both from brands with functionally infinite budgets, both launched into the same cultural moment. The difference between them is taste.

In 2022, two days after Serena Williams announced her retirement, Nike released something strange and beautiful. They'd spent a year feeding archival footage from her entire career into machine learning models — analyzing decision-making, shot selection, recovery patterns, the way her body moved at seventeen versus thirty-five. Then they staged a match: Serena from her first Grand Slam at the 1999 U.S. Open versus Serena from the 2017 Australian Open. Not a deepfake. Not a gimmick. A year-long study rendered as sport. The avatars played 130,000 games, enough tennis to stream for an entire year back-to-back. The final match was broadcast on YouTube and watched by 1.7 million people.

What made it work wasn't the technical achievement, though that was considerable. What made it work was that someone at Nike or AKQA had asked the right question: what does this technology let us see that we couldn't see before? The answer wasn't "a faster way to make an ad." The answer was: we can watch an athlete's evolution across decades, collapsed into a single match, and understand something about mastery and change that statistics alone can't capture. The AI served the story. The humans had figured out what story was worth telling.

Now consider Coca-Cola's 2024 Christmas ad. The company partnered with three AI studios to remake its iconic 1995 "Holidays Are Coming" commercial — the one with the red trucks rolling through snowy streets, the one some people say marks the start of the Christmas season. The AI version featured trucks, snowy towns, smiling people in scarves. All the signifiers, none of the warmth. Critics called it "soulless," "devoid of any actual creativity." One influencer dissecting bad ads on instagram said it had "no idea what the Christmas feeling is all about." Alex Hirsch, the creator of Gravity Falls, wrote that Coca-Cola is "red because it's made from the blood of out-of-work artists."

Again, the backlash wasn't about the visuals being slightly uncanny. For instance, the wheels on the trucks were gliding instead of turning and the faces slid into that AI blankness. It was about what the choice revealed. Here was a company whose entire brand is built on nostalgia and authenticity — "the real thing," "real magic" — using synthetic generation for an ad that was supposed to evoke human connection. The AI hadn't served the story. The AI had contradicted it.

What's interesting is that Coca-Cola's CMO defended the decision by saying AI cut production time from a year to a month. As if speed were the point. As if the Christmas ad were a deliverable to be optimized rather than an annual ritual that people actually care about. They'd solved for efficiency and missed the point entirely which is that any good advert needs to make people feel something.

Our content marketing tool generates fifty subject lines in thirty seconds. I watched my colleague stare at all fifty last week, his cursor hovering over one and then another, his face slowly acquiring the expression of someone who's been asked to choose their favorite grain of sand.

"They're all fine," he said finally.

"Yeah," I said. "That's the problem."

The default response to this kind of paralysis is to optimize for metrics. Click-through rates, engagement scores, conversion percentages — numbers that offer the comfort of apparent objectivity. But metrics can't tell you whether your brand voice is slowly becoming indistinguishable from everyone else's. They can measure whether someone clicked, but not whether what they clicked on made their day slightly better or slightly worse.

One team I know has developed what they call the Tuesday morning test. Before publishing any AI-assisted content, they ask: If someone encountered this on a Tuesday morning while drinking bad office coffee and scrolling through their phone on the subway, would it add something to their life or take something away? It's a small question. But it requires a kind of judgment that no amount of technical training can provide.

The campaigns that get AI right tend to share a quality I can only describe as knowing what they're optimizing for, and it's never just completion of a task.

In 2017, Nutella did something quietly brilliant. They fed an algorithm a database of patterns, colors, and shapes — all within their brand guidelines — and generated seven million unique jar designs. Not a single duplicate. Each jar was stamped with its own authentication code, like a work of art. They called it Nutella Unica. The jars sold out in a month.

What Nutella understood was that the AI could do something a human design team couldn't: create genuine uniqueness at scale. Not fake uniqueness, not "personalization" in the marketing sense of addressing someone by their first name. Actual, irreducible singularity. Every jar on the shelf was the only one like it in the world. The algorithm served the feeling of specialness that Nutella wanted to create. The humans had figured out what feeling was worth creating.

Or take Sephora's virtual artist, which uses deep learning trained on tens of thousands of skin images to recommend products tailored to individual skin tones and concerns. The AI is doing the unglamorous work of matching and predicting, freeing up the humans to focus on the actual relationship with the customer. The technology recedes into infrastructure. What remains is a person standing at a makeup counter, feeling understood.

I've started asking myself, before I open any AI tool, what I'm actually optimizing for. What voice am I trying to maintain? What would feel authentic versus adequate? What's the difference between content that sounds like it came from my team and content that sounds like it came from the ether? These are harder questions than "how do I write a better prompt." They require knowing something about yourself — your aesthetic, your values, the texture of the thing you're trying to build. Without that, AI becomes a sophisticated way to generate perfectly adequate mediocrity at scale.

I think about a Turkish grocery store I visited once on Green Lanes in North London, where the woman at the register had puffy lip filler and bleached hair and could tell you exactly which bus lines had shifted their routes since the new flats went up on the corner. She was specific. She was irreducible. She couldn't have been generated by anything.

The best work has that quality. The quality of having emerged from a set of experiences and constraints and obsessions that no optimization function could replicate. The question is whether we can recognize it when we see it — and whether we can protect it when everything around us is pushing toward the generic.

I don't have a framework for this. I'm suspicious of frameworks in general; they tend to flatten the very thing they're trying to preserve. But I do think AI literacy, for people who don't work in technical roles, is less about learning new tools and more about getting clearer on what you value. What does your work sound like when it sounds like you? What would be lost if it stopped sounding that way?

I don't know if taste can be taught. But I think it can be practiced — in the choosing, in the refusing, in the willingness to sit in a conference room and say, out loud, that none of these options feel right, even if you can't yet articulate why.
